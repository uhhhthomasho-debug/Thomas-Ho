## A FAREWEL TO AMERICA. TO MRS. S. W.

# A Literary Reading of Phillis Wheatley Peters’ “A Farewel to America”

## Introduction

  This reading of Phillis Wheatley Peters’ poem “A Farewell to America” is a reflective exploration of her relationship with eighteenth-century America, romanticized through natural imagery, particularly flowers, and allusions to Greek mythology. The imagery and atmosphere throughout the poem are indicative of Wheatley’s complicated relationship with America, as an enslaved African woman who experienced the oppressive social structures of colonial society while simultaneously developing her passion for poetry through her education. This relationship is particularly significant when considering Susanna Wheatley, whose family enslaved Phillis but also provided her with an education that allowed her to develop her literary abilities. The flowers that “boast their gaudy pride” contribute to the romanticized atmosphere of the poem, reflecting Wheatley’s emotional attachment to the geographical environment which she occupies but is preparing to leave (Wheatley 6). The significance of this imagery lies in its contrast with the oppressed and marginalized identity of her life in America, creating a complex relationship between personal attachment and the broader realities of enslavement.

## Natural Imagery and Emotional Attachment to New England

  One might assume that an enslaved woman departing eighteenth-century America would express an overwhelming sense of joy at leaving an environment defined by racial oppression. However, Wheatley instead establishes an atmosphere of emotional departure and emotional attachment to the land she is leaving behind. Although the surviving historical records offer limited insight into the complexity of Wheatley’s personal emotions and relationships, the language of the poem suggests that she developed a meaningful connection to New England despite undergoing abolition. This interpretation is particularly evident in the opening line, where she writes of “New England’s smiling meads” (1). The personification of the landscape as “smiling” establishes a romanticized geographical environment, presenting the land through an affectionate rather than hostile perspective. This is further reinforced through her descriptions of flowers, birds, and gardens, particularly the phrases “flow’rets rise,” “feather’d warblers sing,” and “garden blooms.” These expressions emphasize the natural beauty of New England, suggesting that Wheatley associates the environment with memories and emotional attachments that extend beyond her experiences of enslavement. To further reinforce this atmosphere of melancholy, Wheatley introduces Susanna as an important emotional figure in the poem. She writes, “Susannah mourns” and describes the sorrow accompanying “sad departure’s hour” (13, 16). The imagery of Susanna’s grief emphasizes the personal relationships that Wheatley associates with her life in America. This moment is particularly significant because it complicates the relationship between an enslaved woman and the family responsible for her enslavement. Although Susanna played an important role in Wheatley’s education and literary development, this relationship existed within the unequal power structures of slavery. Nevertheless, Wheatley’s depiction of Susanna’s grief suggests an emotional connection that she is reluctant to leave behind. The sorrow expressed through this interaction complements the romanticized descriptions of nature, reinforcing the idea that Wheatley’s attachment to America is shaped by both the people and the geographical environment she has come to know. Rather than presenting her departure through the lens of liberation and freedom, she depicts her departure as a mournful and emotional event. Ultimately implying that through this reading, the poem “A Farewel to America” by Phillis Wheatley Peters is an expression of her attachment to New England the literary upbringing it provided her despite its institutional slavery.

## Greek Mythological Allusions

Greek mythological allusions remain a pervasive feature of Phillis Wheatley’s poetry, demonstrating her classical education while also contributing to the emotional atmosphere of “A Farewell to America.” One particularly important example occurs when Wheatley personifies *Health* as a celestial woman adorned with the mantle of *Hebe*, the Greek goddess associated with youth. The connection between Health and Hebe symbolically unites the virtues of youth, vitality, and physical well-being, all of which become especially significant when considering Wheatley’s earlier references to her declining health. This reference and connection between Health and Hebe is especially significant considering it is located subsequently after the mention of the ocean as Wheatley writes:

> And with astonish’d eyes explore  
> The wide-extended main

(30-31)

This is significant because it signifies an ambivalent relationship she has between her mournful departure for New England and her anticipated arrival to London. The poem operates initially in the emotional mood of the departure but is juxtaposed with this anticipation of London accompanied with the reintroduction of Health. This is particularly significant from a biographical perspective because Wheatley’s familiarity with classical mythology was developed through the education she received in America. Therefore, even as she prepares to depart from New England, her poetic expressions remain influenced by the intellectual environment that shaped her literary identity. This imagery is subsequently developed through the introduction of *Aurora*, the Greek goddess of the dawn, and *Phoebus*, associated with the sun. These mythological references establish the physical setting of Wheatley’s journey toward London while simultaneously communicating her anticipation of reaching her destination. Aurora is associated with the morning light surrounding London, although the city remains obscured by mist and clouds. Wheatley then appeals directly to Phoebus, questioning why his chariot moves so slowly and requesting that his rising rays reveal the city. This moment is particularly significant because the movement of the sun becomes symbolic of Wheatley’s desire to reach an unfamiliar destination. Her impatience for daylight reflects her anticipation of London, while the obscured landscape may also suggest the uncertainty associated with entering a new environment. The mythological references therefore serve a purpose beyond demonstrating her classical education, as they provide a poetic expression of Wheatley’s emotional state during her journey. These allusions ultimately imply that despite her mournful departure from New England, she is anxious to discover the unknown and be in London.

The conclusion of Phillis Wheatley’s “A Farewel to America” signifies her acceptance of departure and the resolution of the emotional ambivalence established throughout the poem. This reading suggests that Wheatley ultimately resolves the juxtapositions between her mournful departure from New England and her anticipation of arriving in London. She begins the poem by stating:

> For thee Britannia, I resign  
> New England’s smiling fields

Poetically expressing her willingness to embrace Britain’s unfamiliar landscape while continuing to romanticize the land she is leaving behind. The word “resign” is particularly significant because it suggests her willingness to relinquish her attachment to New England and accept the new reality awaiting her in London. Although Wheatley initially expresses sorrow over leaving behind a familiar environment, her language gradually shifts toward acceptance and anticipation. This emotional transition becomes especially significant in the concluding section, where Wheatley communicates a newfound sense of determination and spiritual clarity. Earlier in the poem, Wheatley personifies *Health* to express her physical condition and emotional desires, and this same literary technique is employed in the conclusion through the personification of *Temptation*. She writes:

> But Thou! Temptation hence away,  
> With all thy fatal train  
> Nor once seduce my soul away

(41–43)

This establishes Temptation as an opposing force that threatens her emotional and spiritual stability. Her direct rejection of Temptation suggests a newfound sense of determination and clarity, contrasting with the emotional uncertainty established earlier in the poem. Furthermore, the commanding language of “hence away” demonstrates Wheatley’s refusal to allow this opposing force to influence her state of mind, reinforcing her movement toward emotional resolution.

This opposition is further emphasized in the concluding lines:

> Thrice happy they, whose heav’nly shield  
> Secures their souls from harms,  
> And fell Temptation on the field

(49–51)

The imagery of a “heav’nly shield” symbolizes spiritual protection, while the defeat of Temptation suggests the triumph of virtue over internal conflict. The religious imagery also reflects Wheatley’s Christian beliefs, establishing spiritual conviction as a source of strength during her transition between America and Britain. Temptation therefore functions as a personified moral threat whose rejection symbolically parallels Wheatley’s resolution of her earlier emotional ambivalence. Ultimately, the conclusion can be interpreted as Wheatley embracing her departure with a renewed sense of acceptance, spiritual conviction, and anticipation for the experiences awaiting her in London. The juxtaposition between Wheatley’s mournful departure from New England and her anticipation of London creates an emotional tension throughout the poem. Although she expresses sorrow over leaving the land and relationships that shaped her upbringing, she simultaneously looks forward to the possibilities that London represents. This contrast is particularly important when considering how Wheatley associates New England with natural beauty and personal attachment, while London is introduced through celestial imagery and the promise of new experiences. Her appeal to Phoebus demonstrates a desire to move forward, even as her earlier descriptions of New England reveal an attachment to the place she is leaving behind. The transition between these settings therefore reflects more than a physical journey across the Atlantic; it also represents an emotional movement between familiarity and uncertainty. Ultimately, Wheatley’s use of natural imagery and Greek mythological allusions establishes an emotional narrative in which sorrow and anticipation coexist. The flowers, birds, and landscapes of New England represent her attachment to the environment in which she developed as a poet, while the mythological figures of Hebe, Aurora, and Phoebus illustrate her desire for renewed health and anticipation surrounding her arrival in London. What makes this poem particularly compelling is that Wheatley does not portray America exclusively through the oppression she experienced as an enslaved woman, nor does she present her departure as an entirely joyful occasion. Instead, her romanticized imagery and mythological references reveal the complexity of her relationship with America, suggesting that personal affection and the realities of enslavement can coexist within her poetic representation of the land she is leaving.

# Historical and Biographical Context of Phillis Wheatley’s “A Farewel to America”

## Historical Context and Publication

Phillis Wheatley’s “A Farewel to America” reflects upon her experiences in eighteenth-century America and the emotional circumstances surrounding her departure from the country in which she was raised and developed her literary identity as an enslaved African poet. An important historical distinction when interpreting this poem is its date of composition, May 7, 1773, whereas her actual departure for London likely occurred on May 8. According to Mukhtar Ali Isani in her article “Wheatley’s Departure for London and Her ‘Farewel to America,’” the poem was published in Boston and subsequently received recognition beyond the city through its circulation in various colonial newspapers (123–24). The poem appeared in Boston newspapers on May 10, 13, and 21, with each publication containing slightly different versions of the text. Despite these variations, Isani argues that the publications contained no substantial differences and “presented essentially one version” (123). This distinction is particularly important because it demonstrates how Wheatley’s departure was represented to contemporary American audiences through a relatively consistent poetic expression of farewell. The poem’s circulation extended beyond Boston, appearing in *The Essex Gazette* in Salem on May 11 and *The Connecticut Courant* in Hartford in the following weeks (Isani 124). Its publication across different colonial newspapers demonstrates the public attention surrounding Wheatley’s literary accomplishments and her departure for Britain. Isani further explains:

> There was even a report that she was bound for England in response to an invitation by the Countess of Huntingdon.

(Isani 124)

This report reflects the recognition Wheatley had acquired through her poetry and the public curiosity surrounding her journey. Such historical circumstances are significant when considering the emotional atmosphere established throughout “A Farewel to America,” particularly because her departure represented an important transition in both her personal life and literary career. Although Wheatley remained enslaved at the time of her departure, her growing reputation as a poet had already extended beyond the geographical boundaries of New England. Another important historical consideration is Wheatley’s relationship with Susanna Wheatley, whose family enslaved her and facilitated her education. Wheatley directly references Susanna in the poem, describing how she “mourns” at the prospect of her departure. This expression of grief suggests an emotional attachment between the two women, complicating the relationship between Wheatley’s experiences of enslavement and the personal connections she developed in America. The inclusion of Susanna is particularly significant because it reinforces the mournful atmosphere of the poem, suggesting that Wheatley’s departure involved leaving behind meaningful personal relationships rather than simply escaping an oppressive environment. Most importantly, the distinction between the poem’s composition on May 7 and Wheatley’s probable departure on May 8 provides an essential framework for interpreting its emotional atmosphere (Isani 123). Rather than understanding the poem as a reflection written during her voyage across the Atlantic, it can be interpreted as an expression of her emotional state immediately before leaving America. This contextual distinction is particularly relevant to the juxtaposition between her mournful descriptions of New England and her anticipation of arriving in London. Wheatley’s descriptions of the geographical landscape, her relationship with Susanna, and her mythological references to the anticipated arrival in Britain can therefore be understood as expressions of both emotional attachment and uncertainty about her future. Ultimately, understanding the historical circumstances of the poem’s composition and publication provides a framework through which Wheatley’s conflicting emotions can be interpreted, revealing how her departure represented a transition between the familiarity of New England and the uncertainty of London.
